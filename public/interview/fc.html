Flying Cars (AirHop)

A browser racing game where the car sticks to floors, walls and ceilings and spends one tank of air on tricks.

30-second pitch

Flying Cars is a single-player 3D racing game that runs in the browser. Suction holds the car to any surface so tracks can climb walls, run along ceilings and loop, and everything special draws from one shared air tank: hops, boosts, flips and dashes. You race three AI drivers on three tracks and try to beat your own ghost. It's TypeScript, Three.js and Rapier, with no backend and no asset files.

2-minute walkthrough

  • One air tank of 100 units. Hop costs 15, boost 20 per second, air control 3 per second, cushion burst 5. You earn air back: +10 per full flip, +5 for a perfect landing, +4 per second regenerating on a surface, +40 per second on charge strips.
  • The core tension is spending air now to go faster against saving it for a gap or a tight corner.
  • Three tracks (Warm-up, Spiral Tower, Gap Run), three AI racers, three laps, per-lap ghost of your best time, best times saved in localStorage.
  • Every gap is a hole with a 4 m ledge: you can hop it or drive the ledge slowly, so every track is finishable without hopping, only slower.
  • HUD is designed for glances: vertical air gauge (red and pulsing under 20), speed, lap, position, ghost gap with sign, pop-ups like PERFECT / ROUGH / CRASH.

Architecture

keyboard -> Input object -> surface.ts (find surface + normal)
   -> forces.ts / thrusters.ts (gravity, suction, drive, air)
   -> Rapier step (fixed 1/120 s)
   -> Race.ts (gates, laps, positions, ghost)
   -> render/ and ui/ read the new state
  • physics/ and track/ never import render/ or ui/. That is why Sim.ts can run headless in tests.
  • Every tuning number lives in util/constants.ts; no allocation inside the physics step (reuse vectors and quaternions); orientation stored as a quaternion, never Euler angles.
  • The keyboard and the AI both fill the same plain Input object, so the AI drives through exactly the same physics path as the player.
  • A fixed-timestep accumulator decouples physics from frame rate.

Hard problems and what I learned

  • Falling forever in a gap. A checkpoint gate sat inside a hole, so respawning put the car over the hole again, in a loop. Fix: gates slide back until there is 30 m of solid road ahead on road flatter than 20%, with a test checking every gate of every track.
  • Ray leaking through a mesh seam. The centre line of the road is a seam between triangles, so a ray exactly on it could exit the underside and read 1 m too far, which kicked the ride damper. Fix: cast three rays (centre plus two a hair apart) and take the closest hit.
  • Camera never turned. A slerp used the same object as input and output, which silently did nothing. Fixed and covered by a test.
  • First physics step missed the ray structure. Rapier builds it on the first step, so the car "landed" with a free speed boost. Fix: one extra step in the constructor.
  • Grip limits flat corners. On a 60 m radius turn at 55 m/s the car slides about 7.5 m outward. Corners must be wide, banked or the grip retuned.
  • AI struggled on twisting road. A car off-centre sits on a surface moving fast enough that suction cannot follow, so the AI limits road turn rate and avoids boosting there.

Limitations to state honestly

  • Keyboard and desktop only; needs WebGL.
  • A human can still fall off a twisting section at speed; the fix options are a longer twist or a spring that follows surface motion.
  • No car-to-car collisions.
  • Bundle is large because of Rapier's embedded WebAssembly.
  • Tuning is by feel, so playtesting feedback matters.

What I would do next

  • Gamepad support and a mobile control scheme.
  • Shared ghosts or a leaderboard (needs a small backend).
  • Car-to-car interaction if bumping is wanted.
  • Trim the bundle with lazy-loaded WebAssembly; add more tracks and a track editor on top of the existing spline builder.

Likely questions

How does the car stick to a ceiling?

Every step a ray finds the nearest surface and its normal. The game applies suction along that normal, strong enough to beat gravity near the surface and fading with distance, and applies gravity itself pointing toward the surface, with a spring holding ride height.

Why a fixed timestep?

Determinism and stability. Physics at 1/120 s no matter the frame rate keeps behaviour consistent, makes tests reproducible and makes ghost replay accurate. Rendering interpolates from the latest state.

How do you test a game?

Physics and track logic are pure TypeScript with no DOM, so Vitest runs them headless: landing classifier, flip counter, gate placement for every track, a test autopilot that completes laps. Feel is checked by playtesting.

How does the AI work?

Waypoint-following driver with a precomputed speed profile from track curvature, a backward braking pass, and a skill factor. It hops 8 m before gaps and uses the ledge when hops are off.

How did you keep 60 fps?

Fixed particle pool of 1400 with no allocations during racing, no heavy blur over the canvas, no per-step object creation in physics, and no asset loading.

How does the ghost work?

The best lap is recorded as a pose every 1/30 s plus exact samples at lap start and end, stored in localStorage. The gap is computed by comparing times at equal distance covered.

How do tracks get built?

A "Pen" builder lays out splines with straights, turns, rises and gaps. Road frames and banking are computed, the up vector is made consistent, and one welded slab mesh is generated per track. Closed tracks are fixed so the road returns exactly to its start.

Be ready to be honest about

Be upfront that Claude Code wrote most of the code and you directed it: you wrote the spec, made the product and architecture calls, tested on real devices and reviewed the output. Interviewers respect a clear account of what you decided and why far more than a claim you typed every line.
